and its systematic uncertainty}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This note describes, step by step, the model of the gluon-fusion di-Higgs cross section
implemented in \code{dihiggs/smooth.py}. It replaces the October 2026 version of this note,
which described the neural network \code{HEFTNet}; the network is now a cross-check
(\cref{sec:crosscheck}). The model predicts the density $d\sigma/d\mhh$ as a function of
the di-Higgs mass $\mhh$ and of five HEFT Wilson coefficients. The dependence on the
coefficients is a known polynomial with 23 terms. Each of the 23 unknown functions of $\mhh$
that multiply those terms is a smooth spline. Because the model is linear in its parameters,
its posterior density is \emph{exactly} Gaussian. No training, ensembles or sampling are
needed. The note gives particular attention to the model's \emph{systematic uncertainty}: an
uncertainty that comes not from statistical fluctuations but from an imperfection of the
physics description. Here the imperfection is that the POWHEG spectra depart slightly from
the 23-term form that the theory says they must have
(\cref{sec:systematic}).
\end{abstract}

\begin{keyidea}[In brief]
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The model.} $\displaystyle \frac{d\sigma}{d\mhh}(m;\theta) =
    \sum_{i=1}^{23} C_i(\theta)\,a_i(m)$, where the $C_i(\theta)$ are the 23 known HEFT
    monomials and $a_i(m) = \phi(m)\sum_{j=1}^{40}\beta_{ij}B_j(m)$, with $B_j$ cubic
    B-splines and $\phi$ the two-body threshold factor. The 920 coefficients $\beta_{ij}$ are
    fitted to the \emph{bin integrals} of 3,989 POWHEG spectra by Poisson maximum likelihood,
    with a smoothness prior whose strengths are set by the evidence.
  \item \textbf{Three uncertainties} enter any comparison with POWHEG: the Poisson noise of
    the POWHEG sample (statistical, $\approx$ 1,000 events per bin near threshold); the fit
    uncertainty of the model (statistical, a median 2.9\% of the noise); and the
    \textbf{model-error term} (systematic).
  \item \textbf{The systematic.} POWHEG departs from the 23-term form smoothly in $\mhh$, and
    most where the 23 terms cancel. We model the departure as a relative error $\varepsilon(m)$
    on each term, giving a standard deviation $\delta = \varepsilon(m)\sum_i|C_i(\theta)A_i|$
    per bin. $\varepsilon$ is measured on data not used in the fit: 0.08\% at threshold,
    0.28\% at 600~GeV, 3.7\% at 1.45~TeV (three parameters in all).
  \item \textbf{Validation} on 1,000 independent spectra. The accuracy equals that of the
    best possible model of the 23-term form (likelihood-ratio statistic $-2\ln\lambda$ per bin
    1.566 below and 1.100 above 555~GeV). With the systematic included, the pulls have
    standard deviation 1.004 and are calibrated at every degree of cancellation.
\end{enumerate}
\end{keyidea}

\section{What we want to model, and why}
\label{sec:goal}

In gluon fusion, $gg \to hh$, new physics described by HEFT changes both the size and the
shape of the di-Higgs invariant-mass spectrum. The relevant part of the HEFT Lagrangian
is~\cite{alasfar}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{HEFT}} \supset
  -m_t\Big(\ct\,\frac{h}{v} + \ctt\,\frac{h^2}{v^2}\Big)\bar t t
  - \kl\,\frac{m_h^2}{2v}\,h^3
  + \frac{\alpha_s}{8\pi}\Big(\cggh\,\frac{h}{v} + \cgghh\,\frac{h^2}{v^2}\Big)
    G^a_{\mu\nu}G^{a,\mu\nu},
  \label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
where $v \approx 246$~GeV. Each coefficient has a simple meaning:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
coefficient & interaction it controls & SM value\\
\midrule
$\kl \equiv c_{hhh}$ & Higgs self-coupling $hhh$ & 1\\
$\ct$   & top Yukawa coupling $t\bar t h$ & 1\\
$\ctt$  & contact coupling $t\bar t hh$ & 0\\
$\cggh$ & effective (point-like) coupling $ggh$ & 0\\
$\cgghh$& effective (point-like) coupling $gghh$ & 0\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
We collect them into $\theta = (\kl, \ct, \ctt, \cggh, \cgghh)$.

We want the density $d\sigma/d\mhh(m;\theta)$, valid everywhere in a broad region of the
five-dimensional $\theta$ space, together with an honest uncertainty. Such a function lets
us \emph{reweight} existing simulated events to any other $\theta$, instead of running a new
simulation for every point. It is also an ingredient of the simulation-based construction of
confidence sets in $\theta$ that motivates this project~\cite{lee,alkadhim}.

\Cref{fig:spectra} shows four of the simulated spectra. They are very different: their
sizes span three orders of magnitude, some fall steeply from threshold, one peaks near
700~GeV, and spectrum~(b) has a sharp dip near 330~GeV. The dip is a sign of destructive
interference between different contributions to the amplitude. The model has to describe
all of these with one set of parameters. Interference will matter again when we come to the
systematic uncertainty (\cref{sec:systematic}).

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{spectra.pdf}
  \caption{Four POWHEG spectra from the held-out test set, chosen to show the range of
  shapes. Left: cross section per 15~GeV bin (log scale). Right: the same spectra divided

  \label{fig:spectra}
\end{figure}

\section{The physics: why $\sigma$ is a polynomial in $\theta$}
\label{sec:physics}

\subsection{The amplitude is linear in the couplings}

At leading order (LO) there are five classes of Feynman diagrams for
$gg\to hh$ in HEFT (\cref{fig:feynman}). Each class contains exactly one
combination of couplings: two top Yukawa vertices in the box, a Yukawa vertex
and the self-coupling in the top-loop triangle, and so on. Therefore the full
amplitude is a \emph{linear} combination of five fixed sub-amplitudes,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{M} = \ct^2\,\mathcal{M}_1 + \ct\kl\,\mathcal{M}_2 + \ctt\,\mathcal{M}_3
  + \cggh\kl\,\mathcal{M}_4 + \cgghh\,\mathcal{M}_5
  \;\equiv\; \sum_{i=1}^{5} v_i(\theta)\,\mathcal{M}_i ,
  \label{eq:amp}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  \bv(\theta) = \big(\ct^2,\; \ct\kl,\; \ctt,\; \cggh\kl,\; \cgghh\big).
  \label{eq:v}
\end{equation}
The sub-amplitudes $\mathcal{M}_i$ depend on the kinematics (on $\mhh$ and the
scattering angle) but \emph{not} on $\theta$. All of the $\theta$ dependence is in the
five numbers $v_i$.

\subsection{Squaring the amplitude gives 15 terms}

A cross section is proportional to $|\mathcal{M}|^2$. Squaring
\cref{eq:amp} gives
\begin{equation}
  |\mathcal{M}|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{5}\sum_{j=1}^{5} v_i\, v_j\,
  \mathrm{Re}\big(\mathcal{M}_i\mathcal{M}_j^*\big).
\end{equation}
Now integrate over everything except $\mhh$ (the scattering angle, and the
parton distributions). Because the $v_i$ do not depend on
the kinematics, they come out of the integral:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\sigma}{d\mhh}(\mhh,\theta) = \sum_{i\le j} v_i(\theta)\,v_j(\theta)\, K_{ij}(\mhh),
  \qquad
  K_{ij}(\mhh) \propto \int \mathrm{Re}\big(\mathcal{M}_i\mathcal{M}_j^*\big)\,
  d(\text{everything but } \mhh).
  \label{eq:quadratic}
\end{equation}
$K$ is a symmetric $5\times 5$ matrix of functions of $\mhh$, so it has
$5\cdot 6/2 = 15$ independent entries. The 15 products $v_iv_j$, written out in the
couplings, are the first 15 monomials in \cref{tab:monomials}. The diagonal
entries $K_{ii}$ are squared magnitudes and so are non-negative; the off-diagonal
entries are \emph{interference} terms and can have either sign. Negative
interference is what produces dips such as the one in spectrum~(b) of
\cref{fig:spectra}.

\subsection{Next-to-leading order adds 8 more terms}

The training data were generated with POWHEG at next-to-leading order (NLO) in
QCD. At NLO new diagram classes appear, and the polynomial grows from 15 to 23
terms~\cite{alasfar}. One way to see where the 8 extra terms come from is to
consider the NLO diagrams in which each Higgs boson attaches to the gluons through
its own single-Higgs vertex, either a top loop ($\propto\ct$) or the point-like
$ggh$ vertex ($\propto\cggh$). Their coupling factors are
$\boldsymbol{w} = (\ct^2,\;\ct\cggh,\;\cggh^2)$. Interfering these with the LO
amplitude gives products $w_a v_i$. The products with $w_1=\ct^2$ reproduce LO
monomials, but those with $\ct\cggh$ and $\cggh^2$ give exactly the 8 new monomials
16--23 of \cref{tab:monomials}.


\begin{keyidea}[Key idea]
For \emph{every} $\mhh$ the density is a fixed polynomial in $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\frac{d\sigma}{d\mhh}(m;\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{23} C_i(\theta)\, a_i(m)
  = \bC(\theta)\cdot\ba(m)\;}
  \label{eq:factorised}
\end{equation}
The 23 monomials $C_i(\theta)$ are \emph{known exactly} (\cref{tab:monomials}). Only the 23
functions $a_i(m)$, in pb/GeV, are unknown. They are the integrated, NLO-corrected
interference kernels $K_{ij}$. The coupling dependence and the kinematic dependence
\emph{factorise}.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{assume}[When is the factorised form exact?]
The derivation needs only two things. First, the amplitude must be linear in the five
coupling combinations of \cref{eq:v}, together with their NLO extensions. Second, nothing
else in the calculation may depend on $\theta$: the renormalisation and factorisation
scales, the parton distributions and the phase-space cuts must be the same for every
$\theta$. In a fixed-order calculation that meets these conditions, \cref{eq:factorised} is
exact, not an approximation. This matters later: if the POWHEG spectra depart from the
23-term form, the departure tells us about the calculation or its numerical implementation,
not about missing physics in the form itself (\cref{sec:systematic}).
\end{assume}

The LHC Higgs Working Group determines the $a_i$ (called $dA_i$ there) one bin at a time with
a least-squares fit~\cite{alasfar}. Here they are smooth functions of $\mhh$, determined by
all bins at once.

\section{The data: POWHEG spectra are histograms of counts}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Spectra.} The file \file{data/powheg_total_gauss_params_13TeV.csv.gz} contains
4,999 POWHEG NLO spectra at $\sqrt{s}=13$~TeV. Each row holds one parameter point $\theta$
and the cross section in pb in 100 bins of 15~GeV ($[15k,\,15(k+1))$~GeV). The points are
scattered roughly as Gaussians centred on the SM point, with standard deviations of about 5
for $\kl$ and about 2 for the other four coefficients. We use the 80 bins from 255 to
1455~GeV. A thousand spectra are set aside untouched as the \emph{test} spectra. The
remaining spectra are ``diced'' into one row per bin $(\theta_s, k, t_{sk})$ and shuffled.
88\% of these rows (281,044, from 3,989 spectra, including 1,814 restored empty bins) are used
in the fit. The other 12\%, the 38,076 \emph{quick-test} rows, are never used in the fit and
serve to measure the systematic uncertainty (\cref{sec:eps}).

\paragraph{Exact counts.} Every spectrum is a histogram of 40,000 generated events of equal
weight $q_s$ (a few incomplete runs have fewer; \file{data/README.md}). The cross section in
bin $k$ of spectrum $s$ is therefore \emph{exactly}
\begin{equation}
  t_{sk} = q_s\, n_{sk}, \qquad n_{sk}\in\{0, 1, 2, \dots\},
\end{equation}
and \code{dihiggs/counts.py} recovers $q_s$ and the integer counts $n_{sk}$ from the stored
numbers by a greatest-common-divisor search (\file{docs/counts/counts_note.pdf}). The counts
are Poisson distributed and, apart from a negligible multinomial correlation, independent.
Near threshold a bin holds about 1,000--1,800 events; at 1.4~TeV, about 20. This gives us
the exact statistical noise of every number we fit, which is what makes the separation of
statistical and systematic uncertainty in \cref{sec:systematic} possible.

\section{The model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Smooth coefficient functions}

Each coefficient function is a sum of $J = 40$ cubic B-splines times a threshold factor:
\begin{equation}
  a_i(m) = \phi(m)\sum_{j=1}^{J}\beta_{ij}\,B_j(m), \qquad
  \phi(m) = \sqrt{1 - \frac{4m_h^2}{m^2}}, \quad m_h = 125~\text{GeV}.
  \label{eq:spline}
\end{equation}

\begin{aside}[B-splines in brief]
A cubic B-spline $B_j(m)$ is a smooth bump. It is a piecewise cubic polynomial that is
non-zero only over four consecutive knot intervals, and its pieces join with continuous first
and second derivatives. A weighted sum of B-splines can represent any smooth curve whose
wiggles are not finer than the knot spacing. Each coefficient $\beta_{ij}$ acts locally: it
moves $a_i$ only near knot $j$. B-splines are the standard tool for smoothing in
statistics~\cite{deboor,eilers}.
\end{aside}

\paragraph{Knots.} The functions change fastest just above threshold and slowly above
600~GeV. The 38 breakpoints are therefore spaced uniformly in $\sqrt{m - 250~\text{GeV}}$
between 250 and 1500~GeV, which makes them dense just above threshold (\cref{fig:basis}). The
knot vector is \emph{clamped}: each end point is repeated three more times. As a result the
40 basis functions sum to one everywhere on $[250, 1500]$~GeV, and only $B_1$ is non-zero at
250~GeV.

\paragraph{Threshold factor.} At $\mhh = 2m_h$ the two-body phase space vanishes like the Higgs
velocity in the $hh$ rest frame, $\phi = \sqrt{1-4m_h^2/m^2}$, and so does $d\sigma/d\mhh$.
Building $\phi$ in leaves the splines only the smooth remainder to describe. Within
255--1455~GeV the factor hardly changes the fit. Below 255~GeV it is decisive: predicting the
unused 240--255~GeV bin, the model with $\phi$ is 8\% high, the model without it 55\% high.

\subsection{The prediction is a bin integral, and it is linear}

POWHEG gives the integral of the density over each bin. The model's prediction for bin
$k = [e_k, e_{k+1}]$ of a spectrum at $\theta$ is therefore the integral of
\cref{eq:factorised} over the bin, not its value at the centre:
\begin{equation}
  f_k(\theta) = \int_{e_k}^{e_{k+1}}\frac{d\sigma}{dm}\,dm
  = \sum_{i=1}^{23} C_i(\theta)\,A_{ik}, \qquad
  A_{ik} = \int_{e_k}^{e_{k+1}} a_i\,dm = \sum_{j=1}^{J}\beta_{ij}\,I_{jk}, \qquad
  I_{jk} = \int_{e_k}^{e_{k+1}}\phi\,B_j\,dm .
  \label{eq:linear}
\end{equation}
The numbers $I_{jk}$ depend only on the knots and the binning. They are computed once by
16-point Gauss--Legendre quadrature in each bin (\code{SplineBasis.integrals}). The prediction
is \emph{linear} in the $23J = 920$ coefficients $\beta_{ij}$. Everything that follows (an
exact posterior, exact predictions for any binning, an exact evidence) rests on this
linearity.

\paragraph{The free fit as a limit.} If each basis function is a box covering one bin, and
$\phi = 1$, then \cref{eq:linear} becomes a separate fit of 23 numbers per bin, $23\times80 =
1{,}840$ in all. This is the Working Group's per-bin approach~\cite{alasfar}. No model of the
form \cref{eq:factorised} can describe the data better than this free fit, so we use it as a
benchmark. It reappears in \cref{sec:systematic} as the key piece of evidence.

\section{The fit}
\label{sec:fit}

\subsection{Likelihood: weight by the model, not by the data}

With hundreds of events in most bins a Gaussian likelihood is accurate:
\begin{equation}
  -\ln p(\boldsymbol t\mid\bbeta) = \tfrac12\sum_{s,k} \frac{(t_{sk} - f_{sk})^2}{\snoise^2(s,k)}
  + \text{const},\qquad \snoise^2(s,k) = q_s\,\max(f_{sk},\, q_s).
  \label{eq:like}
\end{equation}
The variance is the Poisson variance \emph{of the model's prediction}, $q_s^2\,\mu_{sk}$ with
$\mu_{sk} = f_{sk}/q_s$ the predicted number of events. The $\max$ protects the few bins where
the prediction is below one event. Weighting by the \emph{observed} count instead (Neyman's
$\chi^2$, $\snoise^2 = q_s t_{sk}$) gives downward fluctuations larger weights. It biases the
fit low by about one event per bin~\cite{bakercousins}, which, with 3,989 spectra pooled and
20--50 events per bin at high mass, amounts to many standard deviations. Because
$\snoise^2$ depends on the fit, the fit is iterated: fit, recompute the variances, refit. Each
step moves half-way, by the geometric mean of the old and new variances, to avoid
oscillation (\code{SmoothHEFT.fit\_poisson}, 12 iterations). At convergence this is the
Poisson maximum-likelihood fit. A toy with known coefficient functions confirms that the fit
recovers them within its quoted uncertainties (\file{code/smooth_toy.py}: pulls with mean
$+0.06$ and s.d.\ 0.97).

\subsection{Smoothness prior and the evidence}

For each function $i$, a Gaussian prior on the second differences of its coefficients (a
``P-spline''~\cite{eilers}):
\begin{equation}
  -\ln\pi(\bbeta_i\mid\lambda_i) = \tfrac12\lambda_i\sum_{j=3}^{J}
  \big(\beta_{ij} - 2\beta_{i,j-1} + \beta_{i,j-2}\big)^2 + \text{(negligible ridge)} + \text{const}.
\end{equation}
This is the discrete analogue of a penalty on the curvature $\int (a_i'')^2\,dm$. Straight lines
are not penalised. The 23 strengths $\lambda_i$, one per function because the functions
differ by orders of magnitude in size, are chosen by maximising the evidence
$p(\boldsymbol t\mid\boldsymbol\lambda)$ with MacKay's fixed-point updates~\cite{mackay}.
Because the model is linear, the evidence is available in closed form.

\subsection{The exact posterior}

Likelihood and prior are both Gaussian in $\bbeta$, so the posterior is exactly Gaussian:
\begin{equation}
  \bbeta \mid \boldsymbol t \;\sim\; \mathcal N\big(\hat\bbeta,\; H^{-1}\big), \qquad
  H = \Phi^{\mathsf T}W\Phi + \Lambda, \qquad \hat\bbeta = H^{-1}\Phi^{\mathsf T}W\boldsymbol t,
\end{equation}
where each row of $\Phi$ is $\bC(\theta_s)\otimes\boldsymbol I_k$ (\cref{eq:linear}),
$W = \mathrm{diag}(1/\snoise^2)$, and $\Lambda$ is the prior precision. The factorisation into
monomials and bins makes $\Phi^{\mathsf T}W\Phi = \sum_{k=1}^{80} S_k\otimes\boldsymbol
I_k\boldsymbol I_k^{\mathsf T}$, with $S_k = \sum_s w_{sk}\bC_s\bC_s^{\mathsf T}$ a $23\times23$
matrix, so the whole fit takes about a minute on a laptop CPU.

\paragraph{Predictions.} For any $\theta$ and any interval, the prediction is
$\boldsymbol g^{\mathsf T}\hat\bbeta$ and its \emph{fit variance} is
$\sfit^2 = \boldsymbol g^{\mathsf T}H^{-1}\boldsymbol g$, with $\boldsymbol g = \bC(\theta)\otimes
\int\phi\boldsymbol B\,dm$ over that interval. Correlations between bins, between values of
$\theta$, and between the $a_i$ come out automatically. The fit uncertainty is small, a median
2.9\% of the Poisson noise of a POWHEG bin: the 3,989 training spectra determine the 23
functions very well. This is \emph{not} the dominant uncertainty, as the next section shows.

\section{The systematic uncertainty: the model-error term}
\label{sec:systematic}

\subsection{Three kinds of uncertainty}
\label{sec:three}

Compare the model's prediction $f_{sk}$ with the POWHEG cross section $t_{sk}$ in one bin of
one spectrum. Three distinct effects make them differ:

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{p{3.2cm}p{2.0cm}p{5.3cm}p{3.6cm}}
\toprule
uncertainty & kind & origin & size (median, relative to the noise)\\
\midrule
Poisson noise, $\snoise$ & statistical & finite number of events in the POWHEG sample being compared;
  belongs to the \emph{reference}, not to the model & 1 (by definition)\\
fit uncertainty, $\sfit$ & statistical & finite number of training spectra; the posterior
  width $H^{-1}$ & 0.03\\
model-error term, $\delta$ & systematic & POWHEG does not exactly obey the 23-term form of
  \cref{eq:factorised} & 0.17 below and 0.16 above 555~GeV; up to 1 where the 23 terms
  cancel\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The first two are \emph{statistical}: they come from random fluctuations, they shrink as
samples grow, and we know their size exactly (\cref{sec:data,sec:fit}). The third is a
\emph{systematic} uncertainty in the sense physicists use the word. It is not caused by
statistical fluctuations. It is caused by a deficiency in the physics models, here in the
agreement between the POWHEG calculation and the functional form we impose on it. More
training spectra would not reduce it. The three add in quadrature in the pull,
\begin{equation}
  z_{sk} = \frac{t_{sk} - f_{sk}}{\sqrt{\snoise^2 + \sfit^2 + \delta^2}} ,
  \label{eq:pull}
\end{equation}
which should be a standard normal variable if all three are right.

\begin{watch}[What this systematic is \emph{not}]
The model is built to reproduce POWHEG. Its systematic uncertainty measures how well
\emph{POWHEG agrees with the 23-term form}, i.e.\ the internal consistency of the
simulated spectra. It does \emph{not} include the uncertainties of the NLO prediction
relative to nature: renormalisation and factorisation scale variations, parton distributions,
the top-mass scheme, missing higher orders. In a physics analysis those must be added
separately, as for any generator.
\end{watch}

\subsection{The evidence: an accuracy floor that no model of the 23-term form can cross}
\label{sec:evidence}

\paragraph{The floor.} A natural measure of how well a prediction $\mu$ (in events) describes
an observed count $n$ is the likelihood-ratio statistic
\begin{equation}
  -2\ln\lambda = -2\ln\frac{\mathrm{Poisson}(n;\mu)}{\mathrm{Poisson}(n;n)}
  = 2\Big[\mu - n + n\ln\frac{n}{\mu}\Big].
\end{equation}
For a correct prediction it follows, asymptotically, a $\chi^2$ distribution with one degree
of freedom (Wilks). No parameter is fitted to the test spectra, so its mean per bin should be 1.
On the 1,000 test spectra it is 1.566 between 255 and 555~GeV and 1.100 above, for the smooth
model. For the \emph{free fit} (23 independent numbers per bin, the best that any model of the
form \cref{eq:factorised} can do) it is 1.559 and 1.105. For the network ensemble it is 1.561
and 1.121 (\cref{tab:accuracy}). Three very different models reach the same floor. The excess
over 1 is therefore not a property of any particular model of the $a_i$. It is a property of
the data: the POWHEG spectra do not exactly have the 23-term form.

\paragraph{What the floor is not.} The excess is not small-count statistics (near threshold the
bins hold over 1,000 events, and Neyman, Pearson and likelihood-ratio measures agree). It is
not the normalisation: the totals scatter about the fit by 0.47\%, as expected from the counts
alone (0.51\%). And it is not the fit uncertainty, which is 3\% of the noise
(\file{docs/modelerror/model_error_note.pdf}).

\paragraph{It grows with cancellation.} Define for each spectrum the \emph{cancellation
ratio}
\begin{equation}
  R_s = \frac{\sum_k t_{sk}}{\sum_k\sum_i |C_i(\theta_s)A_{ik}|} ,
  \label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
the total cross section divided by the sum of the magnitudes of its 23 terms. $R = 1$ when
all terms have the same sign. $R \ll 1$ when the cross section is a small difference of
large terms, i.e.\ when the interference is strongly destructive. Divide the 1,000 test
spectra into fifths of $R$. \Cref{tab:cancel} shows that the excess, measured by
$\langle z^2\rangle$ with only the statistical uncertainties, sits almost entirely in the most
cancelling fifth.

\paragraph{It is smooth in $\mhh$.} Poisson noise is independent from bin to bin. The residuals
are not: in the most cancelling fifth, neighbouring bins below 555~GeV are correlated by 0.60

\subsection{Why errors grow when terms cancel}
\label{sec:why}

Suppose that in the POWHEG calculation for spectrum $s$ each of the 23 terms carries a small
relative error $\delta_{i}(m;s)$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\sigma^{\text{POWHEG}}_s}{dm} = \sum_{i=1}^{23} C_i(\theta_s)\,a_i(m)\,\big[1 +
  \delta_i(m;s)\big]
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \text{error} = \sum_{i=1}^{23} C_i(\theta_s)\,a_i(m)\,\delta_i(m;s).
  \label{eq:relerr}
\end{equation}
Two features of \cref{eq:relerr} explain everything in \cref{sec:evidence}.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Amplification by cancellation.} The error is of the order of
    $\varepsilon\sum_i|C_ia_i|$, where $\varepsilon$ is the typical size of the
    $\delta_i$, while the cross section is $\sum_iC_ia_i$. The \emph{relative} error on the
    cross section is therefore about $\varepsilon/R$. An error of 0.5\% on terms that
    cancel to 0.25\% of their size is a 200\% error on the result. When the terms add
    ($R\approx1$) the same error is 0.5\%, far below the Poisson noise.
  \item \textbf{The errors must differ from spectrum to spectrum.} If the $\delta_i(m)$ were
    the same in every spectrum, the error would be
    $\sum_i C_i(\theta)\,[a_i\delta_i]$, which is again of the 23-term form with
    $a_i \to a_i(1+\delta_i)$. The fit would simply absorb it, and no floor would appear. A
    floor can arise only if the errors vary from run to run, i.e.\ with $s$, while being
    smooth in $m$ within each run. This is what one expects if each HEFT point was generated
    as an independent run, with its own integration grids and numerical errors.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{aside}[An analogy from the bench]
Measure a small quantity as the difference of two large readings from instruments calibrated
to 0.5\%. A 0.5\% calibration error on each reading is harmless for a large sum but ruinous
for a small difference. Moreover, if the same instruments are used every time, the
calibration error is a fixed offset that can be measured and removed. Only if the
calibration changes from one measurement to the next does it become an uncertainty. That is
the situation here: the ``instruments'' are the 23 components of the POWHEG calculation, and
the ``calibration'' is apparently redone with every run.
\end{aside}

\subsection{The model of the systematic}
\label{sec:model_error}

\Cref{eq:relerr} suggests modelling the systematic as a random error, smooth in $m$, of
relative size $\varepsilon(m)$ on each of the 23 terms. How the $\delta_i$ of one spectrum are
correlated among themselves is unknown. Two limits bracket the possibilities. For the
standard deviation of $\sum_iC_iA_{ik}\delta_i$ in bin $k$, with $A_{ik}$ the bin integral of
$a_i$:
\begin{align}
  \text{independent } \delta_i:\quad & \delta_{sk} = \varepsilon(m_k)\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{23}
    \big(C_i(\theta_s)A_{ik}\big)^2\Big]^{1/2}, \label{eq:quad}\\
  \text{any correlation (upper bound):}\quad & \delta_{sk} = \varepsilon(m_k)\sum_{i=1}^{23}
    \big|C_i(\theta_s)A_{ik}\big| . \label{eq:lin}
\end{align}
The second line follows from the triangle inequality, since the standard deviation of a sum is
at most the sum of the standard deviations. It is the largest standard deviation that relative
errors of size $\varepsilon$ can produce, whatever their correlation. We adopt
\cref{eq:lin}, the more conservative form. It is the \textbf{model-error term}, and it is
added in quadrature to the statistical uncertainties, as in \cref{eq:pull}. The data cannot
distinguish the two forms (\cref{tab:calib}): both calibrate every degree of cancellation,
with a correspondingly larger $\varepsilon$ for \cref{eq:quad}.

\paragraph{Why not simply inflate the noise?} The obvious alternative, a systematic
proportional to the statistical error, $\delta^2 = a(m)\,\snoise^2$, was used earlier in the
project. It fixes the \emph{average} calibration but makes the error the same fraction of the
noise for every spectrum. It is therefore too small for the cancelling spectra and too large
for the others (\cref{tab:calib}, \cref{fig:model_error}c). The form \cref{eq:lin} uses the
physics of \cref{eq:relerr}: it knows, from $\theta$ alone, which spectra cancel.

\subsection{Measuring $\varepsilon(m)$ on data not used in the fit}
\label{sec:eps}

The coefficient $\varepsilon$ is determined on the 38,076 quick-test rows: single bins that
were never used in the fit, about 480 per bin. In each bin $k$ we solve
\begin{equation}
  \Big\langle \frac{(t - f)^2}{\snoise^2 + \sfit^2 + \varepsilon_k^2 S^2}\Big\rangle_{\text{rows in bin }k}
  = 1, \qquad S = \sum_i|C_i(\theta)A_{ik}|,
\end{equation}
for $\varepsilon_k \ge 0$ (by bisection; $\varepsilon_k = 0$ if the statistical errors alone
already give $\langle z^2\rangle \le 1$, which happens in 5 of the 80 bins). Then
$\ln\varepsilon$ is smoothed by a quadratic in $u = (m - 855~\text{GeV})/600~\text{GeV}$,
fitted to the $\ln\varepsilon_k$ with weights proportional to the square root of the number of
rows:
\begin{equation}
  \ln\varepsilon(m) = p_0 + p_1 u + p_2 u^2 .
\end{equation}
These three numbers are the systematic's only parameters. They are stored with the model
(\code{eps\_poly} in \file{smooth_model.npz}) and evaluated by \code{SmoothHEFT.epsilon(m)}.
\Cref{fig:model_error}a shows the result: $\varepsilon = 0.077\%$ at 262~GeV, 0.28\% at
600~GeV and 3.7\% at 1.45~TeV. Near threshold, where the spectra are largest, the 23
components of POWHEG are consistent with one another to about one part in a thousand. That is
a plausible size for the numerical precision of an NLO calculation.

\subsection{Using the systematic}
\label{sec:using_syst}

\paragraph{One bin.} \code{model.spectrum(theta)} returns, for each bin, the prediction and
$\sqrt{\sfit^2 + \delta^2}$; \code{discrepancy=False} gives $\sfit$ alone. To compare with a
POWHEG sample, add its Poisson noise.

\paragraph{Sums over bins.} The model-error term is treated as independent between bins, but
\cref{tab:cancel} shows that the underlying errors are correlated over 5--8 bins. For a
cross section summed over many bins the truth lies between two limits: independent bins,
$\big(\sum_k\delta_k^2\big)^{1/2}$, and fully correlated bins, $\sum_k\delta_k$. For the SM in
255--1455~GeV:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\text{SM}} = 28.11~\text{fb}, \qquad \sfit = 0.006~\text{fb}, \qquad
  0.031~\text{fb} \le \delta \le 0.17~\text{fb} .
\end{equation}
The systematic exceeds the fit uncertainty by a factor of 5 to 30. It is still small (at most
0.6\%), because the SM spectrum is only moderately cancelling overall ($R = 0.25$). Locally,
however, the SM is strongly cancelling near threshold, where the box and triangle diagrams
interfere destructively: the relative systematic is 5.5\% in the 255--270~GeV bin, against
0.6\% at 600~GeV.

\paragraph{In a likelihood.} In an analysis the systematic can be represented as a nuisance
parameter per bin (or per group of correlated bins), with a Gaussian constraint of width
$\delta_k$, in the usual way. This is the frequentist counterpart of the ``model discrepancy''
term of Kennedy and O'Hagan~\cite{kennedyohagan}, which adds to a simulator's output a random
function describing the simulator's own inadequacy.

\subsection{Where does it come from? Open questions}
\label{sec:origin}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The generator.} By the argument of \cref{sec:why}, the departures are smooth in
    $\mhh$ within a run and vary from run to run. A likely candidate is the numerical
    evaluation of the two-loop virtual amplitudes, which in the POWHEG \code{ggHH}
    implementation are interpolated from pre-computed grids, together with each run's own
    Monte Carlo integration. Questions for the colleagues who produced the samples: were the
    HEFT points independent runs with independent seeds and grids? What relative precision is
    quoted for the virtual amplitudes? Were there negative-weight events? Regenerating a few
    strongly cancelling spectra at higher precision would settle the matter.
  \item \textbf{Or the form.} Alternatively, some ingredient of the generation could depend on
    $\theta$ (\cref{eq:factorised} and the box that follows it), in which case the 23-term form
    itself would be incomplete for these samples. A diagnostic: such a departure would vary
    smoothly with $\theta$, so the residual shapes of test spectra close together in $\theta$
    would be similar. Run-to-run numerical errors would make them unrelated.
  \item \textbf{The rise at high mass.} $\varepsilon$ grows to 3.7\% at 1.45~TeV, where the bins
    hold about 20 events. Whether this reflects the generator's precision at high mass, the
    overflow above 1500~GeV, or the limits of measuring $\varepsilon$ from so few events is
    open. It matters little for the cross section, which is small there.
  \item \textbf{Its effect on the $a_i$.} Refitting with $\delta^2$ added to the variances
    (``second pass'') leaves the calibration unchanged (pull s.d.\ 1.003) but fits the
    cancelling spectra less closely ($-2\ln\lambda$ near threshold 1.87 against 1.57). Since
    the purpose of the model is to reproduce POWHEG, the first pass is the primary model. The
    difference between the passes measures how much the individual $a_i$ depend on the
    treatment of the cancelling spectra.
  \item \textbf{Improvements.} A Gaussian-process systematic with amplitude $\delta$ and a
    correlation length fitted to the residuals would replace the two limits above with one
    number for any sum over bins.
\end{itemize}

\begin{watch}[Wide bins and the model-error term]
\code{spectrum} evaluates $\varepsilon$ at the centre of each bin. That is right for bins of
15~GeV or narrower. For a wide bin it is not: one bin from 255 to 1455~GeV gives the SM
$28.11\pm0.73$~fb, because $\varepsilon(855~\text{GeV}) = 0.65\%$ is applied to terms that come
mostly from near threshold, where $\varepsilon$ is up to eight times smaller. For wide intervals, sum
15~GeV bins and combine the $\delta_k$ with the limits of \cref{sec:using_syst} (here
0.03--0.17~fb).
\end{watch}

\section{Cross-checks: the network and the Bayesian network}
\label{sec:crosscheck}

The first version of this project modelled the 23 functions with a neural network,
\code{HEFTNet} (\file{dihiggs/heftnet.py}, \cref{fig:architecture}). The network keeps the exact
polynomial in $\theta$ and learns only $a_i(\mhh) = P_i(\mhh)\,e^{Q_i(\mhh)}$. $P$ is a six-layer
ReLU network with a $\tanh$ output, which supplies shape and sign. $Q$ is one linear layer,
which supplies the overall size and an exponential fall-off. The network is trained with an
L1 loss on the bin values at the bin centres, then fine-tuned with a Huber loss of the pulls.
An ensemble of 20 networks (bootstrap of the training spectra) reaches the same accuracy floor
as the smooth model (\cref{tab:accuracy}). With the same model-error term its pulls are
calibrated equally well (s.d.\ 1.004).

A Bayesian treatment of the network was also studied. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo could not explore
the posterior, whose curvatures span many orders of magnitude, and gave bands of only 0.5\% of
the noise. A mixture of linearised Laplace approximations about the 20 networks gives 2.4\% of
the noise within and 5.6\% between networks (\file{docs/bnn/laplace_explained.pdf}). Like the
smooth model, it needs the model-error term to be calibrated. This is the main lesson of the
cross-checks: Bayes' theorem works, the parameter uncertainty of any 23-term model is genuinely
small, and the dominant uncertainty is the systematic of \cref{sec:systematic}, which no
parameter posterior of a 23-term model can contain.

\section{Points worth noticing}
\label{sec:notes}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Positivity is not enforced.} Nothing forces $d\sigma/dm \ge 0$. On the test spectra
    all 80,000 bin predictions are positive, but near deep interference dips at other $\theta$ it is
    worth checking.
  \item \textbf{Identifiability.} At fixed $m$, the density is linear in the 23 unknowns $a_i$.
    They are determined only if the 23 monomials, evaluated at the training points, are linearly
    independent. With thousands of widely spread $\theta$ points they are, although some
    combinations ($a_{16}$--$a_{20}$) only weakly. With a few points close to the SM they would not
    be.
  \item \textbf{Correlations.} The posterior covariance $H^{-1}$ correlates every prediction with
    every other. For a single bin this does not matter. For sums over bins, or for joint fits of
    several spectra, use $H^{-1}$ through \code{SmoothHEFT.predict} with the summed basis
    integrals, rather than adding per-bin uncertainties.
  \item \textbf{Reproducibility.} There is no training: the same data give the same model to
    machine precision, and the paper figures are regenerated from
    \file{runs/SMOOTH_J40/smooth_model.npz} by the scripts in \file{docs/paper/} (shared code in
    \file{smooth_common.py}).
\end{itemize}
